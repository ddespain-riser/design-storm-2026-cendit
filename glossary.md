# Glossary for the non-water reader

Plain-language definitions used across this repository. Items marked **(general knowledge)** come from general knowledge, not Jake's materials; verify with Denver Water before relying on them.

See also `guide.md` sections 1 to 3 for TOC, alkalinity, soft sensor, turbidity, and specific conductance, and section 10 for R^2, RMSE, MAPE, and MAE.

## Water terms

- **Hydrologic, hydrology (general knowledge).** About how water moves through the land: rain and snow falling, snow melting, runoff into streams, river flow, groundwater seeping out, evaporation. "Hydrologic monitoring data" here means the snowpack (SWE), precipitation, and river flow (cfs) records; "hydrologic events" are the melt flushes, thunderstorms, and drought baseflow that change the river and, with it, TOC and alkalinity.
- **Snowpack.** Snow lying on the mountains through winter. In the Colorado Front Range most of the year's water arrives as snowmelt, so the snowpack is the real reservoir.
- **SWE, snow water equivalent.** If this snow melted right now, how deep a layer of water would it make? Depth of snow is useless (powder vs packed), so SWE is the measurement. A peak of 9.0 means nine inches of water sitting on the hillside as snow.
- **SNOTEL.** The federal network of automated mountain snow stations ("snow telemetry"). Jake used Michigan Creek (station 937) until the Sep 4 update replaced it over bad spring 2026 readings; the shipped replacement CSV matches Hoosier Pass (531) while the notebooks name Buckskin Joe (938). One point in a large watershed: a proxy for the basin, not the basin.
- **Peak and melt-out.** SWE climbs all winter, reaches a maximum (peak), then falls to zero (melt-out). The shape of that curve is the year's water story: high, late, slow means a long strong river; low and gone in two weeks means a short pulse and a thin summer.
- **April 1 SWE.** The traditional comparison date, usually near peak. "How was the snowpack this year" means "April 1 SWE vs average."
- **Water year.** October 1 to September 30. Snow that falls in November melts the following May, so calendar years split one winter in half. Group snow data by water year, never calendar year; grouping by calendar year splits a single snow season across two buckets and makes wet and dry years hard to tell apart.
- **cfs, cubic feet per second.** River flow. 620 cfs means 620 cubic feet of water pass the gage each second.
- **Flush.** The rising melt river scours banks, forest floor, and stream bed and carries a season's accumulated dead plant material downstream in a few weeks. That is the spring TOC spike. "First flush" is the same idea for any storm after a dry spell.
- **Baseflow.** What the river is when it is neither raining nor melting: groundwater seeping from rock. Groundwater has been in contact with minerals, so it carries more of what makes alkalinity. Less melt means less dilution, so a dry year runs higher alkalinity, exactly as 2026 shows.
- **Convective storms, monsoon.** Summer afternoon thunderstorms. In July and August, moist air from the south (the North American monsoon) produces short, intense downpours over small areas. Little annual water, but an inch on one hillside in an hour flushes sediment and organic matter fast.
- **Burn scar (general knowledge).** Land where wildfire removed vegetation; rain runs straight off carrying ash, soil, and organic carbon. Denver Water's watershed has several (Buffalo Creek 1996, Hayman 2002). Ask Jake whether it matters at Strontia.
- **Regime.** A stretch of time where one mechanism dominates. A snow-flush year and a drought-plus-thunderstorm year are two regimes: same river, different physics producing the TOC.
- **Excursion.** A day beyond the operational threshold: TOC above 3 mg/L or alkalinity below 60 mg/L, the days Jake's sample weights emphasise and operators care about.
- **Gage, stage, discharge, rating curve.** A gage is a river measuring station (USGS spells it "gage"; some map names say "gauge"). Stage, or gage height, is how high the water surface sits; discharge is the flow in cfs. Most gages only measure stage and convert it to discharge with a rating curve built from field measurements, so ice, debris, or a shifted channel bends the flow numbers. Reservoirs work the same way: a sensor reads surface elevation and a table turns it into storage.
- **Snow pillow.** The sensor at a SNOTEL site: a pad that weighs whatever snow sits on it. Wind scour, drifting, ice crusts, and thawing sensors all fool it, which is the kind of fault behind the Michigan Creek May 2026 readings.
- **FNU, NTU.** Units of turbidity: how much light bounces off particles in the water. Higher is muddier. The sentinel gage above Strontia peaked at 329 FNU in the August 14 to 15, 2026 storm (provisional, from `strontia-brief/series/`).
- **Provisional.** USGS's label for fresh, unreviewed data. Every real-time reading carries it, and values can be revised later, so a fresh API pull may not match the committed CSVs.
- **Grab sample.** Water collected by hand and tested in a lab. TOC and alkalinity at Foothills are grab samples, which is why the plant learns what arrived only after it arrived, and why a soft sensor is worth building.
- **Influent, effluent (general knowledge).** Influent is water entering the plant (hence `FoothillsInfluent.csv`); effluent is water leaving it.

## Collection system terms (Scenario 3)

Scenario 3 asks how water and water quality move from the watershed through the collection system to the treatment plants. These are the pieces of that journey. Facts about specific structures come from the 3D map's data (`water-system-3d/system.json`).

- **Watershed, drainage basin.** All the land that drains to one point on a river. Rain or melt landing anywhere in it eventually flows past that point, so a snowstorm, thunderstorm, or burn scar anywhere upstream shows up at the gage later. The map draws one basin per gage, from USGS NLDI, in `strontia-brief/basins/`.
- **Runoff (general knowledge).** Rain or snowmelt that flows over or just under the ground into streams instead of soaking deep. "Spring runoff" is the season when the melting snowpack reaches the rivers; storm runoff is the short pulse after a downpour.
- **Mainstem, fork, confluence.** The mainstem is a river's main channel, a fork is a major branch, and a confluence is where two meet. The North Fork of the South Platte, carrying Dillon water, joins the mainstem 2 km above the sentinel gage.
- **Collection system.** Everything that gathers raw water and moves it to the treatment plants: snowfields, rivers, reservoirs, tunnels, and conduits. Pipes that carry treated water to taps are a separate system, distribution **(general knowledge)**.
- **Raw water, treated water.** Raw water is untreated source water in rivers, reservoirs, and conduits; treated water is what leaves the plant. Denver Water's "raw water group" models the collection system.
- **Continental Divide, transmountain diversion.** The Continental Divide is the ridge line where rivers split: west of it they drain to the Colorado River, east of it to the South Platte and Denver. A transmountain diversion tunnels water across it. Denver Water has two: the 23-mile Roberts Tunnel carries Dillon Reservoir (Blue River) water to the North Fork of the South Platte at Grant, and the 6.2-mile Moffat Tunnel carries Fraser River water to South Boulder Creek and Gross Reservoir. Williams Fork Reservoir water repays the west slope for water sent east. So the water at Strontia is not all South Platte snow.
- **North system.** The map's name for the Moffat side: Moffat Tunnel to Gross Reservoir, Ralston Reservoir, and the Northwater Treatment Plant. The rest of the supply comes down the South Platte through Strontia Springs to Foothills and Marston.
- **Acre-foot.** The West's storage unit: one acre flooded a foot deep, about 326,000 gallons. Reservoir storage in the map and in `water-system-3d/storage-history.json` is in acre-feet, by day of water year.
- **Reservoir storage, percent full.** How much water a reservoir holds now, against its capacity. Reservoirs carry wet years into dry ones: they fill after a big snowpack and draw down through drought, so storage lags snowpack by months.
- **Conduit.** A pipe or tunnel carrying raw water. Conduit 26 takes Strontia Springs water to Foothills; Conduit 20 runs by gravity from Waterton Canyon to Marston Forebay.
- **Diversion, weir, intake.** A diversion takes water out of a river; a weir is a low wall across the channel that raises the water so an intake (the opening into a conduit) can draw it. The Conduit 20 Diversion, 2.6 miles below Strontia Springs Dam, is one: Denver Water's name per Cassidi (2026-09-08); OpenStreetMap calls it Marston Diversion Dam.
- **Forebay.** A small pool holding water just before an intake or plant. Marston Forebay has no natural inputs; water is moved there specifically to be treated.
- **Sentinel gage.** USGS 06707525, just above Strontia Springs Reservoir: the last measurement point before 80% of Denver's water enters it. Reads temperature, conductance, dissolved oxygen, pH, and turbidity every 15 minutes.
- **Travel time, residence time.** Travel time is how long water takes to get from one point to the next: about four hours from the sentinel gage to the Foothills intake, per Denver Water's raw water group. Residence time **(general knowledge)** is how long water stays in a reservoir on average. The reservoir mixing and settling what arrives is Jake's explanation for why the plant tracks readings from days earlier, not hours (`guide.md` section 1).
- **Water column, stratification, lake turnover (general knowledge).** The water column is the water from surface to bottom at one spot. In summer the sun warms the surface, warm water floats on cold, and the reservoir settles into layers that barely mix (stratification); the boundary is the thermocline. The bottom layer can run low on oxygen. In fall the surface cools until the layers match, and wind mixes the whole column (turnover), bringing bottom water up. That can change water quality at an intake with no storm at all, which is why Scenario 3 names it.
- **Profiling sonde, cast.** A sonde is a bundle of water-quality sensors left in the water. The Strontia profiling sonde moves up and down through the reservoir; one trip is a cast. It logged 16,093 readings from 2026-04-07 to 08-19 and is the only depth data here, so it is the direct view of stratification and turnover.
- **Chlorophyll, phycocyanin (general knowledge).** Pigments the Strontia sonde measures. Chlorophyll is in all algae, so it tracks how much algae is growing. Phycocyanin is specific to cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), which can cause taste and odour problems and, in some blooms, toxins.

## Modeling and statistics terms

- **Extrapolation.** Answering for inputs outside anything seen in training. Straight-line models try (badly); tree models cannot, and instead return the nearest remembered answer with full confidence.
- **Novelty score.** For one day, how many of its inputs sit outside the training range. A cheap way to flag days where the model is extrapolating.
- **MAE, mean absolute error.** The typical size of a miss in the target's units, as a plain average: each day's miss with the sign dropped, averaged. Unlike RMSE it does not square the misses, so one bad day cannot dominate a group. Used when comparing groups of days (novelty bins, regimes).
- **Median.** The middle value when you sort a list. Half the values are above it, half below. Less sensitive to one extreme value than the mean, which is why the wet/dry reference is a median.
- **Percentile, 5th and 95th.** The value below which 5% (or 95%) of the training days fall. A test day "beyond the 5th or 95th percentile" on some input is in a corner the model saw on fewer than one training day in twenty. Softer than "outside the minimum and maximum", which means never seen at all.
- **Fold.** One train/test split. Rolling-origin folds train on every year before N and test on year N; regime folds train on wet years and test on dry, or the reverse.
- **Anchored rows.** The rule that every feature set is scored on the same days: keep only days where all of Jake's full feature set is present, then remove columns. Without it, dropping a column with gaps changes which days survive, and the comparison is partly a different test set.
- **Spearman correlation.** A number from -1 to 1 saying how consistently two things rise together, based on rank order rather than exact values. 0.4 means "more novelty tends to mean more error, loosely"; 1 would mean perfectly in step.
- **Standardised (z-score).** Each column rescaled so its mean is 0 and its spread is 1, so that inches of snow and days of melt can be compared on one footing before measuring distance between years.
- **Monotonically.** Only ever moving one way. "MAE rises monotonically with novelty count" means each step up in novelty brought a higher error, with no dips.
- **Recall, precision, false alarm, base rate.** Recall: of the days that really were excursions, what share the model flagged. Precision: of the days the model flagged, what share were real. A false alarm is a flagged day that was not real. The base rate is what precision would be if the model flagged every day (59% for alkalinity below 60, since that is how often it happens); precision near the base rate means the flags carry little information. Full explanation in `guide.md` section 11.
- **Lag vs lead time.** Lag is how far back a model's inputs are shifted: 2 days for TOC and 4 for alkalinity in Jake's notebooks (`guide.md` section 6). Lead time is how much warning operators get before the water arrives. Sensors farther upstream give more lead time but a looser link to the plant; the Strontia sonde sits closer, which may sharpen accuracy but shortens the warning, the tradeoff the README flags.
- **Lead (days).** For an episode, the gap between the first day the model flagged it and the day it actually began. Negative means the warning came before the episode; positive means the model noticed after it had started.
